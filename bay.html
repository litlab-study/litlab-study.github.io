Bayonet charge

Summary Quotes Analysed quotes Context Form and structure

Summary

Ted Hughes follows a soldier charging across a battlefield during a war. As he runs, he experiences fear, confusion, and panic, causing him to question the reasons for fighting.

Quotes

"Suddenly he awoke and was running - raw"

"Stumbling across a field of clods towards a green hedge"

"The patriotic tear that had brimmed in his eye"

"Threw up a yellow hare that rolled like a flame and crawled in a threshing circle"

"King, honour, human dignity, etcetera Dropped like luxuries in a yelling alarm"

Analysed quotes

Quote: "Suddenly, he awoke and was running"
The adverb "suddenly" shows how he's in a liminal state between asleep and awake. This contrasts the typical herosism shown through war poems - he is vulnerable and unaware.
The verb "awoke" may also foreshadow how he later metaphorically wakes up to see the futility of war.
The poem starts in medias res - plunging the reader into the battle. This abrupt and disorienting start encapsulates the chaos faced during war.
The repeated adjective "raw" connotes vulnerability and implies that he is unprepared and afraid.

Quote: "Threw up a yellow hare that rolled like a flame and crawled in a threshing circle"
The violent verb "threw" shows how innocent animals are treated cruelly and harmed during war. Alternatively, the phrase "threw up" could link to illness, showing how war has corrupted nature and made it suffer.
The noun "circle" to describe the hare's motion makes it seem as though it's trapped - both the hare and the soldier are caught up in a deadly situation - fighting in someone else's battle.
Colour imagery is used through the adjective "yellow" - connoting hapiness and hope. However, the simile using the imagery of a "flame" symbolises the loss of hope.

Context

Hughes' father was a WW1 soldier so the poem is set in WW1 to show the intensity of war. He most likely wanted to highlight the brutality of war as a tribute to his father's suffering and to warn future generations.

Form and structure

The poem starts in medias res. This is abrupt, recreating the chaos of war. We experience part of the soldier's disorientation and can empathise with him.
Enjambment intensifies the chaos and disorganised tone. Occasional caesura and end-stops create variation, perhaps reflecting how he's trapped and overwhelmed by constant attack.
