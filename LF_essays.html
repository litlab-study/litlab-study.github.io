1. How is Piggy presented in 'Lord of the Flies'?

Throughout the novel, Piggy is presented as the embodiment of rationality, innocence, and wisdom. He is introduced as a polite character and ends up as a “true, wise friend”, showing how he wasn’t as susceptible to the fierce savagery that dominated the other boys. By doing this, Golding reflects how the most empathetic and insightful characters are often ostracised and neglected, effectively creating a microcosm of the WWII society that he faced.

Piggy is textually feminised from the very beginning of the novel. He lived with his “auntie” and his dad was dead, showing him to be vulnerable and likely to be more maternal. He "cradled the conch”, further using maternal imagery to introduce him as a compassionate and collected character. However, women were still viewed as inferior to men during the time of writing, so Piggy’s textual feminisation may foreshadow his noticeable exclusion and the other boys’ disregard for his ideas. From the start, he sticks to the idea that they should all assemble and form a mini-society, perhaps suggesting an innocent naive nature. He is immediately excluded by the older boys; “The boys were a closed circuit of sympathy with Piggy outside”. This metaphor reflects how the other boys shared a deeper understanding and respect for each other, but Piggy was excluded and looked down upon. The sibilance in this quote may be used to emphasise the other boys’ harsh treatment of piggy, highlighting how he is taken advantage of and neglected. As Golding lived in a post-WWII society, he had been scarred by the harrowing events of the war and therefore display in his novel how innocent characters such as Piggy are always the victim to larger, savage powers such as Jack (the allegorical Hitler figure). Therefore, Golding immediately introduces Piggy as an outsider to the other boys, foreshadowing his lonesome end.

As the other boys begin their descent into savagery (as seen through their “painted faces” and growing hair), Piggy clearly clings on to his internal civilised condition; “He was the only boy on the island whose hair never seemed to grow”. Here, the noun “only” further isolates him from the other boys, allowing him to represent a mighty and unique innocence. The symbolism of the boys’ growing hair is used to display their gradual but inevitable venture from civilisation, however Piggy’s inability to leave society behind displays his inherent rational and human nature. Therefore, his name “Piggy” may also act as irony, as he seems to be the most civilised and least inhumane out of all of the boys. This shows that the other boys immediately project their own attitudes onto others when they feel threatened or insecure. However, the verb “seemed” could suggest that Piggy’s hair appeared to stay the same, but perhaps reveal a hidden internal darkening. This shows an oppressed internal conflict within him, perhaps allowing Golding to show the extent of mankind’s innate evil and reflects his attempt to rationalise events rather than confront their moral implications.

Furthermore, Piggy’s rational nature is further reflected as “Any intelligence that had been shown was traceable to Piggy”. Here, the adjective “any” acts as hyperbole, emphasising his immense wisdom and intellect. Therefore, Golding presents how powerful and resilient his wisdom is. The verb “traceable” may also present Piggy as a sort of source of wisdom for on the island, foreshadowing the chaos that erupts as he is pushed aside.

Finally, Piggy is killed by Roger, who “leaned all his weight onto the lever” with a sense of “delirious abandonment”. Here, the use of the noun “lever” could suggest indirect attack, perhaps implying a trance-state as Roger kills him. This could also be a criticism of modern warfare, as mechanisms such as bombs allowed killing to be much more indirect, making it inhumanely easy and emotionless. Through this, Golding warns the readers of the horrors that occur during war and effectively demonstrates the immense indifference humans in power are capable of. After his death, Piggy is said to have “twitched … like a pig that had just been killed”. This shows that even in his final moments, the boys still have not developed respect for Piggy’s wisdom as seen through the dehumanising noun “pig”. This noun also connotes disgust and ridicule, further emphasising the lack of reverence the boys have for him.The verb “twitched” connotes discomfort but also vulnerability, showing how human’s innate evil drives them to attack the weakest in order to prove their dominance. It’s not until the very end that Ralph recognises Piggy as a “true, wise friend” but his eventual realisation of this foregrounds how profound the effect of rationality is on others.

Therefore, Piggy is consistently represented as a source of rationality and guidance throughout the novel. Despite this, he is abused and ridiculed, reflecting how society often ignores true wisdom, suggesting that civilised values are fragile and easily overridden by humanity’s inherent capacity for violence .

2. How is violence/savagery presented in 'Lord of the Flies'?

Throughout the novel, Golding uses the boys’ development and behaviour to explore the theme of violence. By doing this, he intends to display the horrors he witnessed during the Second World War, and the extent to which human beings can inflict harm upon one another. He effectively shines a light on the boys' descent into savagery when the strict restraints of society are removed.

The pig, hunted by the boys, is used as a symbol of innocence and helplessness. The boys chant “Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood”. This chant shows the breakdown of order and the rise of their primal instincts. Their chant is monosyllabic, so the simple structure shows their youth and juxtaposes their brutal intentions, while also having a hypnotic effect. The verb “kill” is imperative and aggressive. It is a command- showing the lack of moral question- and immediately introduces violence and finality. The feminine pronoun “her” and the noun “pig” both represent the boys’ controlling nature and foreshadows the brutal murder of piggy, who was consistently textually feminised (through behaviour such as cradling the conch and defending the littluns). The pronoun “her” also adds a gender dimension and reveals the underlying themes of misogyny and dominance over the feminine. This links to the 1950s gender roles, as this was a time where women were still seen as inferior to men. The verb “spill” and noun “blood” gives the act a ceremonial quality- like a sacrifice. The blood becomes the ultimate symbol of the boys’ overwhelming power and brutality. After this, when they’ve stabbed the pig, Roger exclaims “right up her ass!”. Here, a disturbing layer of sexual aggression has been added, as the pig becomes a symbolic victim of sexual violence. The adverbial phrase “right up” is violently specific, leaving no ambiguity. This implies force and total domination, adding to the graphic cruelty of the scene. The pronoun “her” once again links to their toxic masculinity, transforming the act from just a hunt to sexual abuse, making it deeply unsettling and dehumanising. The vulgar slang “ass” is coarse and graphic, showing a total loss of innocence. It is not just a kill- it has become a mockery and an act of cruel performance.

Furthermore Roger is a character that truly embodies violence and sadism. Unlike Jack, who is violent to claim power, Roger enjoys pain and represents pure cruelty. When piggy is murdered; “Roger, with a sense of delirious abandonment, leaned all of his weight onto the lever”. The adjective “delirious” implies mountainous, ecstasy and a lack of self control- to him, violence is now pleasurable and liberating. The noun “abandonment” shows his moral absence, and that he has completely rejected restraint and rules. Additionally, he “leaned all his weight”, which is indicative of his full commitment with no hesitation. The noun “leather” also distances Roger from the act as he uses indirect violence making it easier to kill without remorse. This could be seen as a metaphor for the technology used during the Second World War, which made killing more impersonal and therefore easier. In the final chapter, when the boys all tried to attack Ralph, “Roger sharpened a stick at both ends”. This shows, their completed descent into savagery, they have now become ritualistic, violent hunters. The noun “stick” symbolises their complete shift from civilization to savagery- it was previously representative of tool for survival, but has now become a weapon and a form of punishment. The verb “sharpened” shows his intent and preparation- last time, a stick sharpened at both ends was used to mount the pig's head as a sacrifice for the beast. Roger intends to do the same to Ralph, as he no longer sees him as a human, but rather as prey.

Moreover, Golding uses the boys to reflect the violent intentions of mankind. As they hunt, Ralph thinks that “the desire to squeeze and hurt was overmastering”. The noun “desire” represents a deep, emotional and instinctual craving, which clearly shows ralph's character development as civilization loses its grip on him. Golding suggests that violence is seductive, even for someone like Ralph who was usually a symbol of order and reason. Ralph's lack of control in this situation is shown through the adjective “overmastering”- it suggests that savagery is stronger than willpower and morality. The verbs “squeeze” and “hurt” show his oppressive and harmful intentions. This moment overall blurs the line between play and quality, showing how quickly civilization can slip away. Then, when Simon is murdered; “there were no words, and no movements but the tearing of teeth and claws”. In this quote, the lack of names and dialogue creates a sense of chaotic violence, making the reader feel disoriented and horrified. The noun “words” represents civilization, so when the boys lose their language, they also lose their moral and social identity. They are now no longer rational individuals- they have become a mindless, savage mob. The violent and visceral verb “tearing” conveys their ongoing, uncontrolled brutality and evokes predatory, animalistic violence. The nouns “teeth” and “clause” show the boys’ animalistic nature. They have become beast-like, exactly what they feared. This encapsulates the story's central message- the real beast is within them. Therefore, it shows violence as it was not a calculated moment, it was ritualistic, instinctive and unplanned.

Finally, Golding portrays the universality of violence through the boys’ key moments of realisation. When with Simon, the Lord of the flies says “you knew, didn't you? I'm part of you?”, which shows that savagery and evil is not taught or created, it is already within us. The pronoun “you” directly addresses Simon, but could also be seen as addressing the reader, making the message more impactful, personal and accusatory. The use of rhetorical questions adds A taunting, sinister tone, making the Lord of the flies seem manipulative. Simon is the allegorical Christ figure throughout the novel, as he defends the marginalised and feeds the littluns. Therefore, this interaction delivers the message of innate human savagery and violence most effectively, as Simon is generally wiser and more emotionally and spiritually mature. At the end of the novel, “Ralph wept for the end of innocence and the darkness of man's heart”. The verb “wept” shows his deep emotional pain, he's mourning the loss of morality, hope and goodness. The “end of innocence” symbolises the loss of childhood purity. Ralph has realised what humans are truly capable of when the structure of society is removed. He is no longer a naive schoolboy, as he has come to understand that the real threat was the for evil within themselves. The noun “darkness” represents the moral corruption within them all. This finalises goulding's message that civilization merely masks this darkness, so when law and order fall away, evil takes over.

Overall, Golding presents savagery as an overpowering force that takes control if given the opportunity. The novel acts as a warning of mankind's capabilities, inspired by the catastrophic events such as the atomic bomb and the Holocaust.

4. How does Golding present leadership in 'Lord of the Flies'?

Throughout the novel, William Goulding uses the boys’ behaviour to create a microcosm of society. By doing this, he displays both despotic leaders, and more marginalised characters, to show the harrowing effects of real life tyrants such as Hitler.

Jack Merridew is immediately introduced as a strict totalitarian leader. He conducts the choirboys in a very airy yet uniformed Manor; “the creature was a party of boys”. The noun “creature” is used to display the choirboys as very united, hinting at Jack's strict leadership methods. This may also foreshadow their upcoming violence towards anyone who does not obey. The noun “party” may also mimic a political party, the political highlighting Later, when voting for a new leader, “with dreary obedience the choir boys raised their hands”, to vote for Jack. As these boys have a pre-established relationship with Jack, this foreshadows his cruelty to those below him. The adjective “dreary” shows their reluctance to vote for him, introducing Jack as the novel's allegorical Hitler figure. The “obedience” reflects the force Jack puts on them, and their lack of personal choice. Therefore, Golding uses Jack to present the threats to society leaders such as Hitler were.

However, this is juxtaposed by Ralph's more nuanced leadership methods. From the start, he displays order by announcing “we've got to have rules and obey them. After all, we're not savages”. The noun “rules” represent structure within their microcosm. Ralph's leadership is not a shock to the reader, as he clearly mimics his father, a naval commander. Ralph's care for structure clearly foreshadows this reason and wit in later chapters, as he desperately tries to “obey” their rules. He says they are not “savages”, which is ironic as the novel documents the boys’ overall descent into savagery. He also stays reasonable and level-headed throughout the novel; “the fire is the most important thing on the island”. The noun “fire” is a symbol of hope, and this illustrates Ralph as a voice of reason, even in such dire circumstances. The determiner “most”, however, shows him as desperate, as he places this hope before everything else. This conflict between Ralph's reason and Jack's impulse builds tension within the novel, showing the disadvantages of many different leadership styles.

Furthermore, the effect of such constrictive leadership is shown when they all chant “kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood”. This chart reflects their mob mentality and the savagery that will arise when society succumbs to such tyrannical leaders. The verb “spill” gives the act a ceremonial aspect, as though the blood is a sacrifice. This may also link to Jack's hubris, as he is in complete control of their ritualistic behaviour. The pronoun “her” builds further on the microcosm of society, as it accurately presents the misogynistic view of women throughout the 1950s. Women were still seen as inferior, and the boys take advantage of this by praying on the most vulnerable. Additionally, as this chant is monosyllabic, it reminds the reader of the boys’ youth. Folding does this to remind the reader that anyone is capable of evil and oppressive leadership, no matter their age.

Therefore, golden presents the boys contradictive leadership methods as dangerous and fraught to harm. The novel acts as a warning to society about the coercive nature of all people, as Golding witnessed himself during catastrophic events such as the Holocaust.
