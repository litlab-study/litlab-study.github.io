London

Summary Quotes Analysed quotes Context Form and structure

Summary

The narrator walks through the streets of London, observing the solemn nature of the city's atmosphere and the people within it. It is set in 18th century England around the industrial revolution.

Quotes

"I wander through each chartered street, Near where the chartered Thames does flow"

"Marks of weakness, marks of woe"

"In every cry of every man"

"The mind-forged manacles I hear"

"Every black'ning church appalls"

Analysed quotes

Quote: "I wander through each chartered street near where the chartered Thames does flow"
The verb "wander" creates a dazed sense, as though the speaker is lost and unfamiliar with his surroundings. This highlights how immensely the city has changed and how far it has fallen into suffering.
Repetition of the adjective "chartered" emphasises the greed of the government, and the metaphor "chartered Thames" shows how they try to exercise their power onto nature aswell.
However, the verb "flow" connotes freedom and tranquility, perhaps suggesting that human power cannot surpress the strength of nature. The river may be a symbol of hope for peace despite the wide-spread oppression.

Quote: "The mind-forged manacles I hear"
The alliteration ("mind" and "manacles") may create a sense of monotonous muttering, reflecting how they suffered silently.
The compound adjective "mind-forged" shows how oppression has been prevalent for so long that it has become internalised. The verb "forged" uses industrial imagery to allude to the industrial revolution. This suggests that the mental chains are manufactured deliberately and systematically.
The metaphor "mind forged manacles" shows how the government has psychologically conditioned people into accepting suffering.
Auditory imagery "hear" emphasises the extent of suffering, as oppression has beccome embedded into daily life.

Quote: "Every black'ning church appals"
Anaphora is used in this stanza, showing how overwhelming the corruption is - every institution is damaged and corrupted.
The verb "blackning" uses colour symbolism to literally represent the physical darkening of infrastructure due to the industrial revolution. However, it may also be symbolic of a moral darkening - reflecting Blake's criticism of institutional religion. William Blake was anti-religion, as he believed the church put on a holy and pure facade whilst neglecting the suffering around it.
The verb "appals" suggests horror and disgust. Instead of comforting and guiding, the church horrifies the speaker. This together with the verb "blackning" creates a sickly semantic field that is sustained all throughout the poem.

Context

William Blake wrote London in 1794, during the Industrial Revolution, a time of great social and economic change. The poem reflects Blake's concerns about the negative impact of industrialization on society, particularly the exploitation of the working class and the loss of individual freedom. Blake lost his faith in religion due to the church's unwillingness to help the poor.

Form and structure:

The ABAB rhyme scheme reflects the relentless, overwhelming and repetitive suffering within the city. It may also create an almost monotonous tone, showing the depressive misery present in the city.
Most of the poem is written in iambic tetrameter (lines of 8 syllables with alternating stressed and unstressed syllables). This meter and the controlled 4-quatrain structure may be used to emphasise the extent of the control the government had over the people of London.
Frequent repetition and anaphora is also used, emphasising the repetitive nature of the suffering caused by corruption.
