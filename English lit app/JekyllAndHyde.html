Summary:

The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde is a Gothic novella written by Robert Louis Stevenson and published in 1886. The story explores the idea that every person has both good and evil within them.
The novella is narrated mainly through the perspective of Mr Utterson, a lawyer who becomes concerned about the relationship between his friend, Dr Henry Jekyll, and a mysterious and violent man named Edward Hyde. At the beginning of the story, Hyde shocks society by trampling a young girl and later murders Sir Danvers Carew, a respected Member of Parliament.
As Utterson investigates, he becomes increasingly worried about Jekyll’s behaviour. Jekyll appears to be hiding a dark secret and becomes isolated from his friends. Strange events occur at Jekyll’s house, leading Utterson and Dr Lanyon to suspect that something is seriously wrong.
Near the end of the novella, it is revealed that Hyde is not a separate person but another side of Jekyll himself. Through scientific experiments, Jekyll created a potion that allowed him to transform into Hyde, a physical embodiment of his evil desires. At first, Jekyll enjoys the freedom Hyde provides, but he gradually loses control of the transformations. Hyde becomes stronger and more dominant, making it impossible for Jekyll to return permanently to his normal self.
The novella ends with Jekyll’s confession, explaining the truth behind the transformations. Unable to escape Hyde’s influence, Jekyll takes his own life, bringing the story to a tragic conclusion.
Stevenson explores themes such as good versus evil, duality, reputation, science and religion, secrecy, and the dangers of unchecked desires. The novella suggests that attempting to separate the good and evil parts of human nature can have disastrous consequences.

Context:

- Published in 1885 - Victorian gentlemen deeply valued their reputation. Respectable men such as Enfield and Utterson avoided things like gossip and prioritised maintaining a put-together exterior.
- The Victorian beliefs in physiognomy (that one's external appearance mirrored their internal character) led many people to believe that a respectable countenance was a definite indicator of a reliable character. However, Stevenson uses Jekyll's respectable appearance to reveal that sinister individuals were able to hide behind polished facades, therefore instilling fear in the Victorian audience.
- Charles Darwin wrote the 'On the Origins of species' a 26 years before Jekyll and Hyde was published. This text shook the pious Victorian society as they feared it separated them from God and would lead them to condemnation.
- The implications of Darwinism and evoution haunted victorian society. They worries about the possibility of humans going back to their primitive state. This worry might be why Stevenson describes Mr Hyde as "ape-like" and "troglodytic". The implication is that the brutal and uncivilised Hyde is somehow a reversion to a more primitive stage of human development.
- The population of London was growing rapidly, poverty struck many areas of the city, and it was rife with crime. Sinister individuals hiding within the crowd became a common trope for gothic literature. Therefore, Stevenson purposely establishes a link between the urban landscape and the dark events surrounding Hyde. The nightmarish imagery creats a sinister landscape befitting the crimes that take place. This makes the novel even more intruigining and thrilling.
