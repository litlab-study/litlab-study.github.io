1. How is the supernatural presented in 'Macbeth'?

Throughout the didactic play “Macbeth” the supernatural is presented through the three witches, who act as catalysts to Macbeth's regicide and eventual downfall. Through this, Shakespeare plays into the prominent Jacobean fears of the supernatural, making the plays more engaging while also pleasing King James I (who was very intrigued by witchcraft).

The play immediately opens on the witches, introducing them to be a key aspect of the play. The pathetic fallacy within this scene reflects Jacobean beliefs that witches influenced the weather, and suggests that their internal moral corruption exudes to negatively affect the environment. The paradoxical quote “fair is foul and foul is fair” uses equivocation to introduce a sense of chaos and disorder into the play the juxtaposition between the nouns “foul” and “fair” foreshadows the prominent theme of deception that will prevail throughout the play, perhaps implying that the witches are responsible for this dishonesty between individuals. This immediate use of equivocation sets the mysterious tone for the play, intriguing many people, including King James the 1st. As he was well known to be interested and mildly frightened by the supernatural (as seen in his book demonology), this portrayal of the witches will have further secured his patronage for Shakespeare. Therefore, the witches are immediately introduced as powerful and influential, foreshadowing that they will be the driving force behind Macbeth's downfall.

Banquo, a “noble” and rational soldier, upon first meeting the witches, refers to them as “withered and wild”. Here, the alliteration creates a hushed and flowing tone, mirroring the ease of the witches’ control. The adjectives connote the witches’ disregard for moral and societal order, posing them as a threat. Banquo also warns Macbeth with the metaphor, “the instruments of darkness... win us with honest trifles to betray us in deepest consequence”. Here, the nouns “instruments” and “darkness” present the witches as mere puppets to a greater more evil force- most likely the devil. The highly pious contemporary audience will therefore have recognised their sordid intentions, making the reader increasingly wary of the witches. The noun “trifles” reflects howthe witches are cunning and manipulative, further amplifyingthe threat that they pose. Banquet's rational nature makes him very reliable, so this will have effectively warnedthe Jacobean audience. The noun phrase “deepest consequence” clearly portraysthe intensity ofthe trouble they cause, effectively demonstratingthe overmastering power ofthe supernatural.

Similarly, when she asks to be “unsex[ed]”, Lady Macbeth calls onthe “spirits who tend on mortal thoughts”. The noun “spirits” could allude tothe Holy Spirit and therefore connote sanctity and purity. This could imply she has been deceived into trusting them, showingthe supernatural's manipulative power. The verb “tend” implies a sense of stalking, emphasisingthe threats that they pose. The separation between these spirits and our own “mortal thoughts” may place them separated from us, suggesting an unnatural imbalance of power between us humans andthe supernatural.

Furthermore, the witches’ is controlling nature is further demonstrated as Macbeth grows increasingly reliant on them; “to the weird sisters... for I am bent to know by the worst means, the worst”. Here, the repetition of the superlative “worst” emphasises their intense cruelty. Macbeth's awareness of this may lie that the witches’ evil is so significant that even someone as evil and brutal as Macbeth can recognise it. Here, the adjective “bent” suggests a sort of mental inversion as though the supernatural has reformed his mind into a way that he can become easily prey to them. This mental defamation foreshadows his psychological decline and places the supernatural as the central force that leads to his downfall.

Finally, by the final act, Macbeth shares that “I begin to doubt the equivocation of the fiend that lies like truth”. This quote links back to Banquo's warning at the start, confirming the harrowing effects that the supernatural can have on humans. The noun “equivocation” would have further amplified the sense of threat that the Jacobeans felt from the supernatural. Post gunpowder plot, many people feared deception and how easily people lied. The association of the witches with this tragic event will have further foregrounded their immense evil and cruel nature. The noun “fiend” to describe the witches is also later used to describe lady Macbeth (“his fiend like queen”), implying A deeper and more profound connection between them. This demonstrates how significantly the witches’ manipulation had an effect on her. Moreover, the juxtaposition between “lies” and “truth” may mirror the conflict caused by the witches their existence and influence. Therefore, Macbeth's eventual and gradual (as he only “begin[s]” to recognise it) realisation of the witches’ malice clearly portrays their callous and calculated nature.

Therefore, Shakespeare uses the witches to catalyse Macbeth's downfall and therefore shine a light on the dangers surrounding the supernatural.

2. How is kingship presented in 'Macbeth'?

Throughout the didactic play ‘Macbeth’ Shakespeare displays both legitimate and illegitimate kingship, through king Duncan and Macbeth. By doing this, he abides by the Jacobean beliefs in the great chain of being and the divine right of kings, warning any nobles of the religious consequences of regicide, and therefore further securing his patronage from King James I.

After hearing of the witches’ prophecies, Macbeth considers regicide but is very hesitant. He uses the simile “his virtues will plead like angels” as a reason against killing Duncan. The personification of the noun “virtues” emphasises Duncan’s wholehearted goodness and pure nature. This shows his moral behaviour, making Macbeth appear even more sinful for taking the life of such a morally just ruler. The noun “angels” places King Duncan right beside God, emphasising his innocence and God-like character. Shakespeare may therefore be constructing Duncan as an idealised model of kingship, designed to reinforce obedience to monarchy rather than literally deifying him. This plays into the Jacobean beliefs of the divine right of kings, which states that kings were believed to be specially chosen by God. This belief is what made regicide such a heinous and unforgivable sin, as the pious contemporary viewed it as a grave violation of God’s natural order. The verb “plead” further reinstates the gravity of Macbeth’s plan, as it suggests a sense of desperate urgency and defencelessness. By placing Duncan in such a vulnerable and innocent position, it further emphasizes his purity compared to the ruthless Macbeth. This portrayal of such a sainted king would have shone a positive light on King James I, allowing Shakespeare to flatter him. Therefore, Shakespeare represents legitimate kingship as something holy and reveals how it can lead to such pure and good rule.

After Macbeth kills Duncan, chaos erupts, and Macbeth recalls to the others what the crime scene looked like; “His gashed stabs looked like a breach in nature”. This simile compares Duncan’s body to a violation of the natural order, revealing how this great chain of being was thought of as the most logical and natural way of life. The adjective “gashed” creates a semantic field of violence and exposure, reflecting how defenceless Duncan was at the time of his murder. The plural noun “stabs” evokes a sense of chaos, mirroring how desperate and uncontrolled Macbeth was during the crime. This sense of disorder and lack of control emphasises how unnatural regicide truly is, and how much both internal and external ruthlessness is required to commit such a sin. Through the use of the noun “breach”, Shakespeare emphasises how nature has been completely disrupted and disregarded by the regicide. The noun “nature” directly links to God and his power, reflecting how Macbeth’s crime is seen as a disobedience in the face of God, and clearly showing the Jacobean audience why his actions are worthy of such condemnation. This suggests that Shakespeare frames kingship as something that is not politically constructed but spiritually enforced, meaning Macbeth’s crime is not just murder but a violation of a cosmic system that guarantees order. By clearly creating this theme of God’s disapproval, Shakespeare actively warns the noble audience against any ideas of regicide, helping King James I post-gunpowder plot. Therefore, Shakespeare creates a clear link between the King and God, in order to emphasise the “sainted” aspect to kingship.

After Macbeth is announced King, Shakespeare explores an entire different side of Kingship, revealing what illegitimate kingship looks like. When Malcolm and Macduff are discussing Macbeth, they refer to him as “This tyrant, whose sole name blisters our tongues”. Both Malcolm and Macduff would be recognised by the audience as noble, honourable, and rational characters, so their reduction of Macbeth to the noun “tyrant” will have been taken seriously. This noun connotes brutality and dictatorship, reinstating how Scotland resembled a “tyrant’s feast” during Macbeth’s rule. The verb “blisters” creates a sense of sickly discomfort, revealing how Macbeth’s tyranny has brought about such immorality, that his name alone induces problems for others. Therefore, this reflects how illegitimate kingship leads to nothing but suffering, turning the audience strictly against regicide.

Finally, by the end of the play, natural order is restored. After Macbeth is beheaded, Macduff proclaims; “Behold… The usurper’s cursed head”. This reduction of Macbeth to a mere “usurper” may suggest that in the eyes of God, he will be no more than the mortal sins he has committed, condemning him to punishment. A cyclical structure is also created as this directly references the beginning of the play, when “[Macbeth] fixed his head onto our battlements”. This cyclical structure reflects the inevitability of the loss illegitimate kingship, and informs the Jacobean audience of the ephemerality of illegitimate power. Malcolm eventually becomes king despite Macbeth’s efforts and, by doing this, Shakespeare further reinstates the belief in the Great chain of being. He reveals that no matter how much humans interfere, the natural order will always prevail and God’s power must not be tested.

Therefore, Shakespeare explores various perspectives of Kingship and uses Macbeth and Duncan to juxtapose each other and foreground the other’s characteristics. Unlike Duncan’s portrayal as divinely sanctioned, Macbeth is increasingly associated with unnatural disruption, highlighting Shakespeare’s contrast between legitimate and illegitimate authority. This allows him to successfully warn against committing regicide and confirm the importance of abiding by God’s natural order.

3. How is guilt presented in 'Macbeth'?

Throughout the didactic play ‘Macbeth’, Shakespeare represents the inevitably of guilt and how it corrodes both Macbeth and Lady Macbeth psychologically. By doing this, he effectively displays the full effect of regicide and usurpation, warning any nobles against it and protecting King James post gunpowder plot (therefore securing his patronage).

Even before he commits the regicide, Macbeth’s destructive guilt is reflected through the imperative; “Stars, hide your fires, let not light see my black and deep desires”. The celestial imagery in this metaphor connotes guidance and hope, and may be associated with God by the pious Jacobean audience. Therefore, his demanding tone is seen as a form of disrespect against God, emphasising the immensity of his cruel nature. The juxtaposition between the nouns “light” and “black” may reflect his own internal conflict as he contemplates regicide. His awareness of his sinful “black and deep desires” reflects how he is innately aware of how wrong his intentions truly are. His desire to be neglected by the stars reflects how he already knows how sacreligious his plan is, foreshadowing his descent into guilt-driven nihilism.

After the regicide, he immediately begins to face the psychological consequences of his actions, as seen in the metaphor; “O full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife”. Here, the exclaimed “O” perhaps physically mirrors his urgency and despair, as though he cries in pain as he is tormented by his guilt. Moreover, the zoomorphism of the guilt as “scorpions” is used to emphasise how he is plagued by the sense of guilt. The imagery of scorpions specifically connotes a sharp, stinging, and lasting pain, foreshadowing how his guilt will completely corrode his previously “brave” persona. Moreover, the adjective “full” highlights how he is incessantly plagued by these emotions, and implies he is too consumed by guilt to think about anything else. This therefore presents the guilt as a destructive and overmastering force that drowns him. Furthermore, his reliance on his “wife” and the term of endearment “dear”, present him as weakened in the eyes of the contemporary audience. During the Jacobean era, there were strict gender expectations, with men praised for their mental clarity and strength, while women were viewed as weaker and inferior. However, Macbeth goes against these expectations here, relying on his wife’s rational nature for support. The contemporary audience would have seen this as a clear indicator of his decaying mental state, as his position as male is being demoted.

Furthermore, later on in the play, the symbol of blood is used for both Macbeth and Lady Macbeth to depict their guilt as a stain against their previous ruthlessness. Macbeth declares; “I am in blood stepped in so far”, using a metaphor to explore how he is engulfed by his guilt. The symbol of blood creates a sickly and unclean feeling, effectively portraying how the guilt stains his moral purity. The choice to replace water (as found in normal rivers) with blood, may be to intentionally represent the heaviness of his guilt, and how it weighs him down. However, the verb “stepped” suggests intent and awareness, perhaps suggesting that Macbeth has come to realise how he is to blame for his own trouble. This reveals that even if accountability is taken, the guilt cannot be washed away. Similarly, Lady Macbeth also demonstrates clear guilt by the last scene of the play, juxtaposing her earlier rationality and indifference (“A little water clears us of this deed”, “Think of it no further”). As she sleepwalks, she hallucinates of her blood-stained clothes; “Out, damned spot, out I say!”. Here, the repetition of the imperative “out” reflects her urgency, implying a sense of hysteria and showing how her guilt strips her of all her cunning and wise qualities. The exclamatory further emphasises her desperate nature.The fragmented exclamatory commands reflect how guilt has shattered her previous composure and authority. Moreover, the adjective “damned” connotes condemnation and punishment, perhaps reflecting her awareness of her own fate. This clearly demonstrates that regicide will inevitably lead to mental decay and instability, effectively warning any nobles of the consequences and deterring them from committing regicide.

Finally, Macbeth also uses similar imperative commands by the end of the play; “Out, out brief candle, life’s but a walking shadow”. Here, the repeated imperative mirrors his wife’s language, reducing Macbeth to the status of a mentally declining woman. The Jacobean audience would’ve seen this as a clear sign of the irreversible extent of his mental deterioration. The reduction of life to something so fleeting using the adjective “but” reflect his newly-adopted nihilistic view, showing how mentally weak he has been made. The adjective “brief” and noun “shadow” create a semantic field of ephemerality, further emphasising his nihilistic approach to life. This effectively demonstrates how guilt can completely destroy even the most “brave” and “noble” individuals.

Therefore, throughout the play, Shakespeare presents guilt as an overwhelming force that leads to inevitable mental corrosion and instability, effectively demonstrating the harrowing and damning effects of regicide.

4. How is Banquo presented in 'Macbeth'?

Throughout the didactic play “Macbeth”, Shakespeare utilises Banquo as a foil to Macbeth, therefore highlighting the extent of Macbeth's ruthlessness. Also, King James the first was rumoured to be a descendant of Banquo, and therefore the positive light on Banquo would flatter King James the first, therefore securing his patronage for Shakespeare.

We are first introduced to Banquo's character when they encounter the witches and he proclaims; “what are these, so withered and wild?”. Here, the alliteration highlights the eerie and unnatural attributes of the witches, playing into the Jacobean fears of the supernatural. Banquets immediate acknowledgement of the malevolence reflects how powerful his conscience and moral compass is. This rhetorical question could also imply A deeper innocence and purity, as he reveals he has no previously established connection with the supernatural at all. Once the prophecies have been delivered, he warns Macbeth; “the instruments of darkness... win us with honest trifles to betray us in deepest consequence”. His early warning and foreshadowing foreground his potent rationality and wisdom. The metaphor “instruments of darkness” uses biblical allusion, perhaps using the “darkness” as a representation of the devil. Banquets characterization of the witches as moose servants to the devil reflects King James the first's own view of the supernatural (as seen in his book “demonology”). By making banquet's character so similar in viewpoint to the king, Shakespeare further flatters him by presenting him as a moral and rational ruler. When the first prophecy proves true, banquet once again questions “what, can the devil speak true?”. His consistent distrust and scepticism towards the witches shows how reasonable and wise he is, juxtaposing Macbeth's blind trust and admiration. This therefore immediately reveals Macbeth's mental instability and his tendency to betray reason.

After the murder of Duncan, Banquo continues to show his scepticism, only this time, towards Macbeth; “I fear thou played most foully for it”. Here, the verb “fear” implies that he is fully aware of the moral implications of regicide, therefore hoping his “partner” is innocent. The adverb “foully” shows how he chid Macbeth for the brutal and sickly nature of his behaviour. Therefore, Banquo acts as a moral anchor who, despite Macbeth's descent into ruthlessness, stays true to his morals and religion. Moreover, his loyalty to Duncan was also shown before the regicide, when he declared “there, if I grow, the harvest will be your own”. This shows banquet's innate loyalty and obedience unto natural order. Banquets respect for the Jacobean “great chain of being” may have set an example for the nobles in the audience, perhaps deterring them from regicide and protecting King James the first. The natural imagery in “grow” and “harvest” further demonstrates Banquo's innocence and purity highlighting the stark contrast between him and Macbeth.

Finally, by the end of the play, as Macbeth's an anagnorisis begins, he will “begin to doubt the equivocation of the fiend”. This realisation directly links to Banquo's initial warning, perhaps implying that Banquo's guidance could have prevented the entirety of Macbeth's downfall. He also comes to accept Banquo's innate rationality; “he hath a wisdom that doth guide his valour”. The noun “valour” shows true Jacobean masculinity, presenting Banquo as an ideal inspiration for Jacobean male society. The noun “wisdom” conveys how Banquo's courage was guided by a deeper intellect and awareness, juxtaposing Macbeth's rash and conflicted bravery. Macbeth's sinful attributes help uplift banquet's purity further, presenting both Banquo and King James the 1st as wise and mature embodiments of masculinity. Macbeth also remembers how Banquo “chid the sisters... when they first put the name of king upon me”, amplifying Banquo's bravery as he dares to speak out against the “devil”. The adverb “first” connotes certainty reflecting how Banquo was immediately aware of the witch's capacity for evil.

Therefore, Banquo's character is sustained as wise rational and loyal juxtaposing Macbeth's tyranny and acting as a model for true Jacobean masculinity.

5. Explore how far Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth as a strong female character.

Throughout the play “Macbeth”, Shakespeare presents lady Macbeth as a Machiavellian villainess, who uses her strong, cunning traits to act as a driving force behind regicide. She abandons her femininity in search of power, as the Jacobean patriarchal society saw strength and femininity as mutually exclusive. However, by the end of the play, she has lost all control and significance, and her strength completely diminishes by her (implied) suicide. Shakespeare may have done this to warn the society further about the consequences of neglecting your natural, gender-based roles.

Upon hearing about the witches’ prophecies, lady Macbeth reveals her bold, strong nature by asking to be rid of her femininity. She commands; “come, use spirits”... “unsex me here”. The imperatives “come” and “unsex” show her confidence and commanding personality. She brutally takes control over the supernatural and this dominance parallels her to the devil. She can order about those “spirits” with no fear of the repercussions, implying moral corruption or perhaps a pre-established relationship with a supernatural. Her connection to those dark forces portray her as hegemonic and would have instilled fear in the audience. Jacobian society deeply feared the supernatural, and King James the first wrote the book “demonology” which clearly stated his hatred for unnatural beings such as witches. Shakespeare uses lady Macbeth to play into these suspicions, and therefore foreground her strength and impact. The verb “unsex” also shows how she wants to rid herself of her feminine traits, which at the time included weakness and obedience, to embody more masculine features such as strength and courage. Therefore, her requests here show her power and complete control.

In the extract, lady Macbeth manipulates Macbeth to convince him to commit regicide. She ridicules him; “when you durst do it, then you were a man”. Here, she undermines his masculinity, and implies she knows more about being a man than he does. In Jacobean society, women were seen as inferior to men, and expected to obey. The distinct mockery of his courage through the verb “dust”, shows her bold and fearless nature, and reveals the lengths she's willing to go through for her ambition. The declarative sentence also further highlights her certainty and confidence, showing her to be strong. Later in this scene, Macbeth tells her “bring forth men children only”. This confirms her inner brutality, as manhood was always considered closely linked to violence. He implies that she is not nurturing (a key feminine trait) enough to care for girls, and is too strong to be a stereotypically correct role model for them.

Buy act four however, the consequences of lady Macbeth's unchecked ambition and unnatural defiance begin to be revealed. She is plagued by guilt after the regicide and demands “out damned spots! Out I say!”. The repeated imperative “out” combined with the exclamation marks show her desperation and loss of hope. This desperation and chaos in her mind shows the psychological effects of committing regicide, and her mental weakness when faced with the guilt. Her demand is not answered, showing her loss of control and lack of strength. The Jacobean society would have noticed her unjust rise to power, and expected her downfall. She strayed from her natural role as a female, and took on too much for her to handle. This signifies the start of things returning to their natural, intended order. Additionally she now speaks in prose instead of iambic pentameter, which further shows her decline in status.

Finally, by the end of the play, lady Macbeth's has lost any strength and significance she had before. When talking about Macbeth and lady Macbeth, she is referred to by Malcolm as “his fiend-like queen”. On a surface level, this simile shows the ruthless and lasting impact she has had on the other characters. She will be remembered for her villainy. However, the possessive pronoun “his” objectifies lady Macbeth, and reduces her to the backbench (like all other women in Jacobean society). She belongs to him and has no individuality, showing her weakness and inferiority. This could also suggest that she will only be remembered for her relationship with Macbeth. As this is in the last scene of the play, it lingers with the audience, acting as a reminder that any act against God's natural plan is pointless. The Jacobean era was a very pious time, so this will have successfully warned against putting on a facade of strength and superiority as a woman.

To conclude, Shakespeare effectively shows lady Macbeth's decline from strong and impactful, to insignificant and weak, to remind the audience of the importance of abiding by our natural, God-given gender roles.
