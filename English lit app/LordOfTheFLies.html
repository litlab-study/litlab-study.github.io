Summary:

Lord of the Flies is a dystopian novel written by William Golding and published in 1954. The novel explores human nature and questions whether people are naturally civilised or savage.
The story begins when a plane carrying a group of British schoolboys crashes on a deserted tropical island during a war. With no adults present, the boys must organise themselves and find a way to survive. Ralph is elected leader, and with the help of Piggy, he tries to maintain order by establishing rules and prioritising the signal fire, which could attract rescuers.
As time passes, tensions develop between Ralph and Jack, the leader of the choirboys. Jack becomes obsessed with hunting and gradually gains power over many of the boys. Fear spreads across the island as the boys become convinced that a mysterious "beast" is living there. This fear contributes to the breakdown of order and reason.
The boys become increasingly savage, abandoning the rules that once kept them united. In a frenzied ritual, they mistake Simon for the beast and kill him. Later, Roger deliberately kills Piggy by rolling a large boulder onto him, destroying the conch at the same time. These events symbolise the collapse of civilisation on the island.
Jack and his followers eventually form their own tribe and hunt Ralph across the island. They set the island on fire in an attempt to kill him, but the smoke attracts a passing naval officer, who arrives and rescues the boys. Confronted with what they have become, the boys are overwhelmed with emotion, and Ralph weeps for the loss of innocence.
The novel explores themes such as civilisation versus savagery, power, fear, human nature, leadership, and loss of innocence. Golding suggests that the capacity for evil exists within all people and that without the structures of society, civilisation can quickly break down.

Context:

Lord of the Flies was written by William Golding and published in 1954, shortly after the Second World War. Golding served in the Royal Navy during the war and witnessed the destruction and violence caused by human conflict. These experiences influenced his view that people are not naturally good and that everyone has the potential for cruelty and violence.
The novel was written during a period when many people were questioning human nature after the horrors of World War II and the Holocaust. Unlike earlier adventure stories such as The Coral Island (1858), which portrayed British boys as naturally civilised and heroic, Golding presents a much darker view of humanity. He suggests that civilisation is fragile and that, without rules and authority, people can quickly descend into savagery.
The novel also reflects concerns about the Cold War and the threat of nuclear war. The boys crash onto the island because of a conflict taking place elsewhere in the world, reminding readers that violence exists not only on the island but also in wider society. Through the island setting, Golding creates a microcosm of society, allowing him to explore ideas about power, leadership, morality, and the struggle between civilisation and human instinct.
