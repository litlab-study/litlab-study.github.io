Jekyll

Quote: "Well-made, smooth-faced man of fifty"


- This shows that he has the time and resources to make himself look presentable.
- His "large face" immediately visualy contrasts Hyde's small phisique, reinforcing the duality between them.
- The compound adjectives "Well-made" and "Smooth-faced" bring about a sense of purity and innocence, a juxtaposition to Hyde with "Satan's signature" on his face.
- Contextually, this links to ideas of physiognomy. Jekyll and Hyde's contrasting appearances imply that "the criminal" can lurk behind an acceptable public persona, making Jekyll and Hyde even more disturbing and instilling more fear and paranoia in the Victorian readers.

Quote: "My master ... is a tall, fine build of a man"


- The possessive pronoun "my" establishes loyalty, but also subservience. Suggests Jekyll is authoritative and respected.
- The noun "master" evokes dominance and power - foreshadowing Jekyll's desire to control his darker self through scientific experimentation. This formal and respectful description could highlight the irony that Jekyll's control is an illusion.
- The adjectives "tall" and "fine" suggest moral uprighteness and elegance (all admirable qualities admired in a victorian gentleman), and contrast Hyde's deformity, symbolising the duality between public virtue and private vice.
- After he says "my master", Poole looks around him and begins to whisper. This shows his uncertainty and paranoia. This could suggest that Jekyll is intimindating, and perhaps threatening towards Poole. It also reveals how unnatural and frightening Jekyll's transformation is as Poole shifts from loyal respect to terrified suspicion.
- Victorian society was obsessed with appearances, class and respectability. Dr Jekyll represents the ideal gentleman; wealthy, educated and controlled. However, the text constantly undermines this perfect facade through the emergence of Hyde, Jekyll's voilent alter ego. Therefore, this is symbolic of how society worships outer perfection whilst hiding inner corruption.
- Stevenson could be using physiognomy (the victorian belief that outward appearance reflected ones inner character) to critique society's obsession with outward respectibility. He may also be warning people that others are not always as they seem, and may be hiding deep sins behind their respectable appearances.

Hyde

Quote: "That child of hell had nothing human, nothing lived in him but fear and hatred"


- Religious imagery - "Child of hell" draws directly on Christian imagery. Hyde is presented as literal spawn of the devil, making him beyond redemption.
- For a Victorian audience, this would intensify the horror, as religion was tightly woven into ideas of morality and evil.
- Dehumanisation - the hyperbole in "nothing human" strips Hyde of his humanity - he is a monstrous outsider.
- Personification of emotions- The fear and hatred that "lived" inside him is his animating force; could suggest that Hyde is not a man, but just a walking vessel of sin and vice.
- Hyde as a "Child of Hell" reflects Victorian fears of damnation and sin.
- After the publication of "origin of species" by Charles Darwin, Victorians feared regression into a more primitive, bestial state. Hyde embodied this fear as he is considered less evolved and even demonic.

Quote: "Jekyll was now my city of refuge"


- This metaphor shows how the two identities are deeply interconnected. It is as though Hyde cannot exist without the facade of Jekyll to shelter behind. This suggests that innate human duality is parasitic.
- The possessive pronoun "my" suggests an inversion of power as Jekyll is no longer in control of Hyde. This contradicts when Jekyll said "The moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr Hyde".
- The noun "refuge" connotes safety and protection, juxtaposing Hyde's violent character.
- Biblical allusion is used to the "cities of refuge" that offered sanctuary to those who committed accidental crimes. Hyde uses Jekyll to shield himself from punishment and evade responsibility.

Quote: "My devil had been long caged, he came out roaring"


- This quote clearly depicts the innate nature of human evil.
- The possesive pronoun "my" shows Jekyll's acceptance that Hyde is not a separate being.
- Religion allusion to the devil is created - Hyde is a manifestation of Sin and temptation, linking Jekyll's actions to the Christian ideas of humanity's fallen nature.
- The animalistic imagery through "roaring" shows Hyde to be savage and uncontrollable. This legitimises the Victorian fears of regression - instilling anxiety in the reader and showing the dangerous reality behind repression.

Secrecy/mystery

Quote: "A great chocolate-coloured pall lowered over heaven"


- Transforms london's atmosphere into a literal funeral shroud, as the noun "pall" represents a cloth spread over a coffin. This metaphorically 'buries' the city beneath moral darkness, suggesting it's contaminated by the same corruption and mystery that characterise Hyde.
- The compound adjective "Chocolate-coloured" introduces a sickly, almost unnatural tinge to the fog, mirroring Jekyll's unnatural experimentation.
- The religious imagery through "heaven" connotes blasphemy, reminding the audience of how such scientific 'heresies' separate us from God.
- The dark landscape creates a sense of concealment and may also build paranoia, as London's unsettling environment is linked to the crimes committed by Hyde. This reflects the Victorian 'urban terror', because the growing city was rife with crime.

Quote: "I beg of you to let it sleep"


- Jekyll acts evasively and he demands to Utterson to ignore the will. His reluctance to trust Utterson suggests he is hiding something to protect his secret alter ego.
- The vagueness of "it" implies that Jekyll is deliberately hiding the true nature of the problem, signalling the extent of secrecy around his "scientific heresies".
- The verb "sleep" hints at the potential to awaken, which may signal that Hyde's presence isn't gone but merely repressed. This indicates that Hyde is metaphorically always within Jekyll, symbolising his veiled evil persona waiting to be revealed.
- This scene also takes place in a private setting after the dinner party, however Jekyll continues to act evasively despite the privacy. This emphasises the extent of the secrecy within the novella.

Utterson

Quote: "They liked to sit a while in his unobtrusive company, sobering their minds in the man's rich silence"


- Utterson is presented stable and the ideal victorian gentleman.
- The adjective "unobtrusive" immediately characterises him as modest, restrained, and socially disciplined. He conforms to ideas of masculine self-control and decorum. He's reassuringly stable for others, and is a safe confidant for his friends. It could also connote suppression - Stevenson may suggest that social respectability depends on concealment.
- The noun "company" implies companionship without emotional intimacy. This reflects the emotionally constrained nature of Victorian male relationships.
- The verb "sobering" implies disciplining thoughts and emotions. Utterson's presence almost acts as a moral corrective, restoring order and rationality.
- The metaphor through the noun "minds" reflects how victorian society prioritises repression over any form of passion or individuality.
- The oxymoronic phrase "rich silence" encapsulates the novella's motif of hidden interiority - what remains unspoken often becomes more meaningful than what is openly expressed.

Quote: "I incline to Cain's heresy. I let my brother go to the devil in his own way."


- The biblical allusion to Cain, the first murderer, perhaps shows how dangerous the Victorian standards of detatchment were.
- The closness of the relationship between "brother[s]" indicates a complex dynamic where Utterson feels responsible for his friend's well-being, yet respects his autonomy.

Quote: "I shall consider it my duty to break in that door"


- The modal verb "shall" conveys determination - signalling his dramatic shift to active intervention.
- This also juxtaposes the previously shown quote as it shows his investment in helping his friend.
- The noun "duty" shows that the horror surrounding Hyde has become so severe that even the embodiment of restraint feels compelled to abandon social etiquette.
- The verb phrase "break in" suggests a forceful action, indicating the extent of his commitment to intervene. It shows both a literal and symbolic force as he breaks through layers of secrecy and repression.
- The possessive pronoun "my" also shows how directly engaged he is in the situation, and how personally he takes the responsibility to act.

Quote: "A lover of the sane and customary sides of life"


- The noun "lover" introduces emotional intensity into a restrained description - it implies devotion and deep attatchment. However, it may also be ironic as his greatest passion is conformity and restriction. Stevenson may satirise the emotional limitations of vicotrian masculinity, where even love is subordinated to social propriety.
- The adjectives "sane" and "customary" create a semantic field of rationality and mental control. Utterson is used as a moral anchor as he abides by the Victorian values despite the chaos that breaks loose.
- The adjective "customary" may also connote an unthinking obediance to convention. Stevenson may be critiquing the ingenuine stoicism in the victorian society.

Quote: "Through wider labyrinths of lamplighted city"


- Directly after first meeting Hyde, Utterson sees him in his nightmares. Stevenson uses nightmarish language to create a strong link between the urban landscape and the dark events surrounding Hyde.
- The noun "labyrinth" suggests that London was inescapable and haunting, so Stevenson successfully utilises the growing city to highlight the mystery and evil all around.
- Plays into the Victorian urban terror. The city was growing rapidly, and so was the divide between the wealth and the poor. The city became rife with crime, so sinister individuals hiding in the crowd became a popular trope for gothic literature.

Science

Quote: "The temptation of a discovery so singular and profound"


- The noun "temptation" establishes a semantic field of religion and sin. This mirrors the story of 'the fall' in Genesis, and implies that the pursuit of forbidden knowledge leads to spiritual corruption. This warns the Victorian readers against unchecked scientific ambition, as they view it as going against God.
- The adjective "singular" may suggest a revolutionary nature behind the discovery. Alternatively, it could also connote isolation; foreshadowing how he will be left separated from society.
- The adjective "profound" may convey intellectual depth, perhaps suggesting arrogance. This may lead the readers to critique his Promethean ambition as he tries to transcend human abilities.

Quote: "Man is not truly one, but truly two"


- The noun "man" universalises the claim - presenting the innate duality in all od mankind. Behind the respectable exteriour lies supressed moral corruption.
- The repeated adverb "truly" conveys absolute certainty. Jekyll displays intellectual arrogance as he tries to scientifically explain everything.
- The balanced syntax of this quote may also reflect the profound duality and clear divide between the two parts of man.
