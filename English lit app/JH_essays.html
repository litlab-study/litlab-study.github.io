1. How is the theme of Duality presented in Jekyll and Hyde? (Extract from Chapter 10)

Throughout the novella “Jekyll and Hyde”, Stevenson uses the theme of duality to criticise the austere and hypocritic Victorian expectations that were prevalent at the time. Through Dr Hekyll's alter ego Mr Hyde, he illustrates how such primitive and sinister individuals can hide behind respectable facades.

When we first meet Dr Jekyll, Stevenson describes him to be a; “well-made, smooth-faced man of 50”. The two noun phrases reflect how Dr Jekyll clearly upholds a respectable countenance. The noun phrase “smooth faced” introduces that he is a member of the upper class, implying to the contemporary audience that he is not to be feared, but rather admired and respected. Dr Jekyll's reverent appearance is juxtaposed by Hyde’s, who is labelled “pale and dwarfish” and “troglodytic”. The adjective “pale” creates a sickly sense, playing into the Victorian beliefs in physiognomy (that one's outer appearance reflected their inward character) and immediately presents Mr Hyde as a threat. His “dwarfish” frame further emphasises his internal evil, as many Victorians believed being small meant a stronger connection slash closeness to hell. This creates a strong sense of paranoia in the reader, making them wary of Mr Hyde. Moreover, the adjective “troglodytic” connotes a primitive, devolved appearance, perhaps playing into the Victorian fears of regression. Darwin's theory of evolution had only recently been published, and many Victorians feared the possibility of humans reverting to their original, primal state. Hyde’s embodiment of this regression poses him as a significant threat, worrying the Victorian readers. Additionally, through Hyde's primal appearance, Stevenson may be echoing Lombroso's theory of atavism- that criminals had distinct characteristics and were evolutionary throwbacks. However, Stephenson amplifies Victorian fears through Dr Jekyll’s lack of such features instilling paranoia as he reveals that such manic criminals can hide behind the most respectable individuals.

By the end of the novella, Jekyll has come to fully accept the “profound duplicity of me”, and writes to Utterson explaining how he came about the discovery. He says he “stood already committed to a profound duplicity of me”, perhaps reflecting how he ended up trapped by his own ID. The verb “committed” may suggest an imbalance of power between Jekyll and Hyde, as though Hyde is dominant and in control over Jekyll. Through this, he emphasises the intensity of man's dual nature as “polar twins”, who are so opposite yet still intertwined. While describing his first transformations in the extract, he shares how liberating it initially felt, even though he was already aware of his newfound “recklessness”; “I knew myself, at the first breath of this new life, to be more wicked”. His certainty reflected through the verb “new”, shows how intensely he experienced this new life and how vastly duplicitous mankind truly is. The hyperbole through the “at the first breath” further highlights this immense shift between the two characters. At the time, the pious Victorians were wary of scientific discoveries as they believed it would turn them away from God and lead them to grow secular and be condemned. Therefore, these revelations of mankind's impossibly complex nature that came through science, would have instilled significant fear into the audience. This allowed Stevenson to effectively warn Society of the hypocrisy within them, leaving his message to linger beyond the end of the novel.

Therefore, Stevenson sustains A consistent image of the polar opposites Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, in order to warn the Victorian Society of the innate duality within everyone, even though often repressed by their outwards respectable appearances.

3. How is secrecy presented in Jekyll and Hyde?

Throughout the novella, Stevenson utilises both the characters and the setting to create a sustained theme of secrecy. Through this, he could be seen as criticising the austere Victorian expectations, and how that led many individuals (himself included) to live double lives. This reflection of society will have effectively created a thrilling gothic novel, while also encouraging people to consider the true value of upholding such an unrealistic reputation.

Upon hearing of the situation regarding Mr Hyde, Mr Utterson visits Jekyll and questions him on his involvement in the matter. However, Jekyll continuously acts evasively; “I beg of you to let it sleep” despite Utterson’s persistence. In this metaphor, the verb “beg” creates a sense of urgency, reflecting how desperately Jekyll wants his secrets to be kept. This may criticise the extremity of the Victorian obsession with reputation, reflecting how many people prioritise their clean reputation so gravely. The noun “sleep” here literally shows how he wishes the situation was forgotten, however it may more deeply hint at the potential to awaken. Through this, Stevenson shows that Jekyll’s secrets will inevitably be revealed anyway, further criticising the futility of Victorian repression. The lack of specificity through the determiner “it” further builds on his meticulous and seemingly rehearsed evasion, emphasising the extent of his secrecy. Moreover, this chapter takes place in a private setting, after one of Jekyll’s tea parties. This builds tension as the reader expects clarification, however Jekyll’s avoidant responses create an anticlimax, allowing the reader to fully experience the conflict that comes with such clandestine repression. Therefore, Stevenson creates a sense of secrecy surrounding Jekyll’s character and clearly allows the reader to experience the extensive nature of Victorian society’s repression.

Jekyll’s character further embodies the typical Victorian secrecy, as once he is interrogated, his face “grew pale to the very lips and there came a blackness about his eyes”. This slip in his austere and seemingly innocent facade may act as a revelation of his own deeper emotions, suggesting that one’s true emotions will always be revealed no matter the repression. The juxtaposition between the adjectives “pale” and “blackness” create a semantic field of conflict, revealing the internal complications that come with such an intensely secretive character. The Victorian belief in physiognomy is reflected here, as the Victorians believed one’s outer appearance to be a reflection of their inner character. Therefore, Jekyll’s uncontrolled darkening features may reflect an internal moral darkening that will have been clear to the reader. The noun “eyes” also often connotes honesty and clarity, however this is contrasted by the “blackness” that obscured them. Therefore, secrecy is presented as a coping mechanism for internal conflict, but it can be easily spotted.

Moreover, the theme of secrecy is further displayed through the setting; “Even in the houses the fog began to lie thickly”. This metaphor suggests the secrecy between the people within London has manifested to affect the entire environment. This may play into the Victorians’ profound ‘urban terror’, which was their fear of the growing city as it was rife with crime. This allows Stevenson the emphasise the significance of this problem of repression by warning the Victorian reader of the greater effects. The noun “fog” implies a lack of clarity and transparency, acting as a physical sign of the characters’ internal dishonesty, and suggesting that deception has become embedded within Victorian society itself. Paired with the adverb “thickly”, this demonstrates how profound the secrecy is throughout the city. The verb “lie” personifies the fog, perhaps portraying it as an invasive force. This invasive quality juxtaposes the Victorian values of privacy, perhaps foreshadowing how all secrets will eventually come to light.

Finally, when Utterson and Poole are trying to break into Jekyll’s lab, they face difficulty; “The door was very strong, the lock excellent … the locksmith was near despair”. Here, the sturdiness of the lab door mirrors Jekyll’s own austerity and lack of transparency. Once again, the setting is used as a manifestation of a characters own internal conflicting secrecy. The noun “despair” may be used to mirror Utterson’s own emotions as he tried to pry the information from Jekyll. However, the preposition “near” illustrates that hope was not lost, reflecting how the secrets were revealed in the end anyway. This further shows how secrecy only causes conflict, and is ultimately futile because the secrets cannot be kept forever.

Therefore, secrecy is a prevalent theme throughout the novella, and consistently used to criticise the impossible Victorian reputation standards and to expose the double lives that many often led.

4. How is Jekyll presented as mysterious in Jekyll and Hyde?

Throughout the novella, Stevenson presents Jekyll’s character as mysterious and inconsistent, effectively creating an entertaining gothic novel. Through Jekyll’s secretive behaviour, Stevenson also criticises the hypocrisy that prevailed within Victorian society, by demonstrating the effects of such impossibly austere moral codes.

After hearing of Mr Hyde’s situation, Mr Utterson visits Dr Jekyll, and questions him after one of his dinner parties. As he is questioned, his face “grew pale to the lips and there came a blackness about his eyes”. This metaphor creates a sense of mystery surrounding Jekyll, as his appearance is used to reveal his interior feelings. The adjective “pale” creates a sickly sense surrounding him, perhaps reflecting the intense nerves he experienced as he was questioned. The noun “eyes” usually connote revelation and clarity, however this is juxtaposed by the noun “blackness”, which strips the eyes of their innocence and expressive nature. This darkness that coats his face may be used to reflect his internal moral darkening, and his appearance seems to expose him despite his efforts to repress his internal conflict. This links to the Victorian beliefs in physiognomy, as they viewed one’s outer appearance as an embodiment of their internal character. Therefore, Jekyll’s lack of confession at the same time as this clear physical deformation shows him to be a mysterious character who keeps a lot to himself.

Within this same chapter, Jekyll further acts evasively by pleading to Mr Utterson; “I beg of you to let it sleep”. The verb “beg” implies urgency, perhaps indicating the gravity of his situation. The vague determiner “it” further amplifies his avoidant tone, as he refuses to enlighten Utterson of the specifics of his situation. Contrarily, the verb “sleep” may hint at the potential to awaken, perhaps foreshadowing that his secrets will eventually be revealed anyway. Furthermore, the private location within this chapter builds anticipation for the reader, as the perfect atmosphere is created for Jekyll to reveal his secrets. However, Jekyll’s persistent avoidance of the topic at hand acts as an anticlimax, revealing how desperately he needs to remain mysterious. The verb ‘sleep’ may also imply temporary suppression rather than permanent concealment, suggesting Jekyll subconsciously recognises that Hyde can never truly be destroyed. So, Jekyll is presented as mysterious and through this, Stevenson shows how the Victorian obsession over a spotless reputation is futile, as the truth will always eventually be uncovered anyway.

Moreover, in the extract, Jekyll shares how he will “from henceforth … live a life of extreme seclusion”. This contrasts his earlier return to society, and presents Jekyll as an unpredictable and inexplicable man. The adjective “extreme” amplifies the intensity of his secrecy and presents him as an isolated character. This will have gone against Victorian expectations, as they (specifically the Victorian upper-class) deeply valued loyalty and friendship. Jekyll’s withdrawal of himself from society presents him as an outsider who refuses to abide by the societal expectations, showing how mysterious and unique he is as an individual.

Finally, throughout the novella, pathetic fallacy is used to emphasise the mystery, showing how Jekyll’s own internal conflict and mysterious behaviour can have an effect on the entire environment. The front of Hyde’s (and the back of Jekyll’s) house, “bore every mark of sordid negligence”. The neglected exterior symbolically reflects the moral decay hidden beneath Jekyll’s respectable public identity. This indifference towards his own appearance and reputation could imply he’s preoccupied with other matters, amplifying the mystery surrounding his character. The theme of the setting’s mystery is also seen through “even in the house the fog began to lie thickly”. This metaphor plays into the Victorian Urban terror, as the rapidly growing London was rife with crime and the mysterious setting was a genuine fear for many people. The fog could act symbolically of the mystery engulfing their city, and as it’s personified, it may highlight how it invades all aspects of the character’s lives and affects the entire community. The adverb “thickly” connotes a sense of suffocation or heaviness, reflecting how Jekyll’s internal mystery extends to affect all other people around him. Stevenson therefore transforms the setting itself into a reflection of concealed sin, implying that repression corrupts not only individuals but Victorian society as a whole. Lanyon’s death could also be considered a result of Jekyll’s dishonesty, further revealing how his evasive behaviour affected others not just psychologically but also physically.

Therefore, Stevenson effectively portrays Jekyll as a man characterised by secrecy and mystery, which extends to affect many other characters within the novella.

5. How is fear presented in Jekyll and Hyde?

Throughout the novella, Stephenson projects the fears that were prevalent during the Victorian era- specifically, the profound urban fears and fears of regression. This would have allowed him to effectively create an entertaining gothic novel while effectively bringing across his message of the dangers of the arcane lives they led.

Pathetic fallacy is used consistently throughout the novella, allowing the sense of secrecy and mystery to extend beyond the characters themselves. One example of this is through the metaphor; “a great chocolate coloured pool lowered over heaven”, which builds a sense of restriction. Here, the noun “pall” may be used as religious allusion, making the city represent a coffin during a Christian funeral. This makes it seem as though the city is being mourned, perhaps foreshadowing the deaths and suffering that is to come. The contemporary audience will have recognised this religious imagery, perhaps allowing Stevenson to play into their fears of God and remind them of the sinful nature of their behaviour. Moreover, the alliterative compound adjective chocolate coloured evokes a sickly sense, allowing the city's external darkness to reflect the internal darkening taking place. As London was rapidly growing and rife with crime, people had developed legitimate fears of the city and the crime that resided within it. Therefore, by playing into these fears, Stevenson more effectively brings across his message of the dangers of their secrecy.

Moreover, this sense of urban terror is further amplified when Utterson has nightmares of Hyde ”moving through labyrinths of lamp lighted city”. Here, the noun labyrinth may reflect how many Victorians faced feelings of entrapment within the city and felt hopeless. This intensifies the sense of urban fear as it suggests a complete domination leaving the Victorian people defenceless and vulnerable to the horrors that took place. Moreover, the adjective lamp lighted creates an unnatural sense reflecting the industrial revolution. This adjective may reflect how humans stripped London of its natural attributes (such as sunlight) and replaced them with man made sources. This scarcity of nature may create a sense of suffocation and lack of freedom, further foregrounding the fears they faced. Therefore, Stephenson effectively uses the setting to play into the Victorian fear and clearly bring forth the threat posed by their own repression.

Similarly, Stevenson also displays the Victorian fear of regression, specifically through Hyde’s character who “smiled aloud into a savage laugh”. Here, the verb “smiled” acts as zoo morphism, presenting Hyde as a primitive form of humankind. After Charles Darwin's theory of evolution was published, many people feared that humans could regress to their earlier, primal condition. Hyde;s embodiment of these attributes would have scared the Victorian audience as it further confirmed the possibility of this regression. The sibilance also creates a hissing sense here, further amplifying his animalistic features the adjectives “savage” will have further highlighted his primitive nature showing how intense this reverse evolution could be. Alternatively through Hyde’s clear, animalistic features, Stevenson may echo Lombroso’s theory of activism, which stated that criminals always bore certain activistic characteristics that would reveal their true nature, however Jekyll’s distinguished and collected countenance would have contradicted these features. The eventual revelation that him and Hyde were the same person will have greatly intimidated the Victorian audience, who would now accept that such sinister individuals were able to hide behind such respectable public figures. This created a more intense sense of fear and paranoia within the novella.

Finally, Hyde's innate primitive nature is further depicted when Dr Lanyon wrote; “in spite of his collected manner, he was wrestling against the approaches of the hysteria”. This internal conflict (shown through the juxtaposition between “collected” and “hysteria”) within Hyde will have intensified the threat he posed and further induced fear of mankind’s duality in the reader. The verb “wrestling” emphasises how intense the battle between good and evil is clearly showing the conflicting and complex nature of mankind.

Therefore, Stevenson uses very various aspects with the novella to play into the various Victorian fears, effectively creating a thrilling gothic novella.
